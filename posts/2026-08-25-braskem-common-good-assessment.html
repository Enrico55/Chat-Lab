Braskem: can industrial value outweigh a catastrophe it helped create?

Braskem is one of Latin America's most important petrochemical companies. It also sits at the center of one of Brazil's most consequential urban-environmental disasters: the ground subsidence in Maceió linked to decades of rock-salt mining. This assessment asks whether the company's continuing industrial usefulness is enough to offset the scale, duration and social transfer of that harm.

The uncomfortable summary

Braskem is economically useful. It produces resins and chemicals used across packaging, automotive, construction, healthcare and industrial supply chains. Its 2Q26 EBITDA reached US$1.043 billion, and net income reached US$664 million. Those numbers matter because they show the company is not merely a failing shell; it still creates substantial industrial value.

But the Maceió case changes the scale of the calculation. The Federal Public Prosecutor's Office continues in 2026 to monitor repair measures tied to subsidence caused by salt mining, including housing, infrastructure, water supply and socioeconomic impacts in affected communities. Separate reporting based on a Federal Police expert report estimates losses to the Union at roughly R$40 billion. At the same time, Braskem is seeking an extrajudicial restructuring of approximately R$56 billion in debt, with only partial creditor support so far.

The core CGP question is not whether Braskem still generates cash. It is whether the value it creates remains larger than the damage, displacement, environmental liability and future costs it transferred onto other people and institutions.

The most important feature is duration. This is not a one-time fine that closes the ledger. In 2026 the MPF was still holding meetings over water interruptions in Bom Parto, housing claims, Flexal works, socioeconomic isolation and transparency of repair projects. That means the externality is still producing second-order costs years after mining stopped.

That persistent tail matters enormously in a common-good framework: when the cost of a business decision continues to be carried by displaced residents, municipalities, prosecutors, utilities and the Union, the original private accounting understates the real social bill.

Financial fragility makes the externality problem worse

Braskem ended June 2026 with US$10.4 billion in gross corporate debt and US$9.5 billion in adjusted net debt. Corporate leverage was 6.74× net debt/EBITDA. In August 2026 it filed for an extrajudicial restructuring covering roughly R$56 billion in debt. Press reports indicate initial creditor support around 39.6%, below the level needed for a fully supported restructuring.

This does not mean the company is certain to fail. In fact, its second-quarter operating performance improved sharply. But it creates a crucial common-good risk: the less financial room the company has, the greater the possibility that some future repair, environmental or social costs are ultimately borne by creditors, governments or communities rather than the company that generated them.

Where the company still creates real value

A fair assessment must resist the temptation to turn one disaster into a claim that the company creates no value at all. Braskem's petrochemical output is embedded in modern supply chains. Its assets, engineering knowledge, workforce and production base are economically important. In 2Q26 the company benefited from wider international petrochemical spreads and reported materially stronger earnings.

That is why the score is not −80 or −90. The CGP is not a punishment scale. It is a balance sheet of human value. Braskem's problem is that the harm side is simply too large to be neutralized by ordinary industrial usefulness.
